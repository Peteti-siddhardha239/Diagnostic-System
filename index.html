📐 Clinical Bayesian Decision Theory Guide

Bayes' Theorem provides the optimal mathematical framework for updating diagnostic beliefs in medicine as new clinical evidence and diagnostic test results arrive.

1. Single-Test Bayes' Theorem Formulation

For a differential diagnosis set of mutually exclusive conditions $D_1, D_2, \dots, D_n$ and an observed test result $E$:

$$P(D_i \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid D_i) \cdot P(D_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} P(E \mid D_j) \cdot P(D_j)}$$
  • Prior Probability $P(D_i)$: The baseline epidemiological prevalence of condition $D_i$ in the patient population before test results are known.
  • Likelihood $P(E \mid D_i)$: The conditional probability that test result $E$ occurs given the patient has condition $D_i$ (Sensitivity for positive result, or $1 - \text{Specificity}$ for false positives).
  • Marginal Likelihood $P(E)$ (Law of Total Probability): The normalizing denominator $\sum_{j=1}^n P(E \mid D_j) P(D_j)$ ensuring all posterior probabilities sum to exactly $1.0$ ($100\%$).
  • Posterior Probability $P(D_i \mid E)$: The updated probability that the patient suffers from condition $D_i$ given the observation of test result $E$.

2. Sequential Bayesian Updating

When multiple diagnostic tests $T_1, T_2, \dots, T_k$ are administered, Bayes' theorem updates iteratively. The posterior distribution from test $k-1$ serves as the prior distribution for test $k$:

$$\text{Posterior}_k(D_i) = P(D_i \mid T_1, \dots, T_k) = \frac{P(T_k \mid D_i) \cdot \text{Posterior}_{k-1}(D_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^n P(T_k \mid D_j) \cdot \text{Posterior}_{k-1}(D_j)}$$

3. The Base Rate Fallacy (Medical Screening Paradox)

Why does a 99% accurate test for a rare disease ($0.1\%$ prevalence) result in most positives being false positives?

Suppose a rare syndrome affects 1 in 1,000 people ($P(D) = 0.001$). A screening test has 99% Sensitivity and 95% Specificity ($5\%$ false positive rate).

In a population of 100,000 people:
  • 100 people have the disease → $100 \times 0.99 = 99$ true positives.
  • 99,900 people are healthy → $99,900 \times 0.05 = 4,995$ false positives!
Therefore, a positive result only indicates a posterior probability of: $$\frac{99}{99 + 4995} \approx 1.94\%$$ A confirmatory test is mandatory to achieve diagnostic certainty!

4. Information Gain & Shannon Entropy in Diagnostics

Dr. Bayes calculates the Shannon Entropy of the differential diagnosis: $$H(D) = -\sum_{i=1}^n P(D_i) \log_2 P(D_i)$$ The optimal test to order next is the one that maximizes the Expected Information Gain (reduction in diagnostic uncertainty): $$IG(T) = H(D) - \mathbb{E}[H(D \mid T)]$$ This allows doctors to pick the most informative and cost-effective test rather than ordering wasteful lab panels.
